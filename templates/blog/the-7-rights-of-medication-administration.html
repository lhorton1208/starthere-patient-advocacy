Why This Matters

Healthcare professionals are trained to follow the 7 Rights of Medication Administration — a foundational safety framework used in hospitals and clinical settings nationwide. But these rights don't belong only to nurses and pharmacists. They belong to you, too.

Understanding them can make you a more empowered advocate for yourself or a loved one, whether in a hospital room, a skilled nursing facility, or at home.

For older adults managing multiple prescriptions across multiple providers, the risk of medication error is not hypothetical — it is ongoing. The sections below explain each of the 7 Rights and provide a practical action step you can take right now.

Right 1: Right Patient

Every medication must be verified for the correct person using at least two identifiers — full name and date of birth — before it is administered. Patient mix-ups happen even in high-quality facilities, particularly when names are similar or a patient cannot speak for themselves.

Tip: Always state your full name and date of birth before receiving any medication — even if the nurse does not ask.

Right 2: Right Medication

The exact prescribed medication must be given — not a similar-sounding drug. Confusion between names like Hydroxyzine and Hydralazine, or Celebrex and Celexa, has caused serious patient harm. Allergy information must also be reviewed before any new medication is introduced.

Tip: Ask "What is this medication and what is it for?" before accepting any drug in a hospital or care facility.

Right 3: Right Dose

The correct amount must be administered. Older adults are especially vulnerable — declining kidney and liver function changes how the body processes medications, meaning a standard adult dose may be too high. Dosing errors can occur at prescribing, dispensing, or administration.

Tip: Ask your provider: "Is this dose appropriate for my age and kidney function?" Any change in dose warrants an explanation.

Right 4: Right Route

Medication must be given the correct way — oral, intravenous, topical, inhaled, injected, or by another designated route. Routes are not interchangeable. An extended-release tablet that is crushed releases its full dose at once, which can be dangerous or even fatal.

Tip: Never crush, split, or alter a medication without first confirming with a pharmacist that it is safe to do so.

Right 5: Right Time

Medications must be given at the correct time and interval. Blood thinners, thyroid drugs, seizure medications, and insulin all require precise timing. Care transitions — ICU to step-down unit, hospital to rehab — are high-risk moments when doses are frequently missed or duplicated.

Tip: At any care transition, request a written, reconciled medication list and compare it carefully to what was given before the transfer.

Right 6: Right Documentation

Every medication given must be accurately recorded — what was given, when, by whom, and at what dose. Without accurate documentation, the next provider has no reliable picture of what has already been administered, raising the risk of duplicate or missed doses, especially at shift changes.

Tip: During any hospital stay, keep a simple medication log noting what was given and when. Good care teams welcome this engagement.

Right 7: Right Reason

The right reason — sometimes called the 7th right — means a medication must be given for a clinically appropriate, documented reason. A patient should always understand why a medication is being prescribed, not just what it is. This right protects against over-medication, inappropriate prescribing, and treatments that no longer match the patient's current condition.

Tip: Ask "Why am I taking this, and is it still needed?" — particularly for any medication that was started during a hospitalization.

Note: Polypharmacy — taking 5 or more medications — significantly raises the risk of receiving drugs without clear ongoing indication. Review your full medication list with your provider at least once per year.

How a Patient Advocate Can Help

A patient advocate does not replace your pharmacist, physician, or nursing team. But an advocate who understands the 7 Rights can ask the questions your care team may not have time to ask — and catch the errors that happen in the gaps between providers, between shifts, and between care settings.
